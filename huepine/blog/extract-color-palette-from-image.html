How to Extract a Color Palette From Any Image (and Actually Get the Hex Codes)

September 2, 2026

You found the perfect photo. The light, the mood, the exact blue in the corner — you want that palette in your design. So you go to pull the colors out of the image, and you hit a wall: the tool wants you to upgrade, or sign in, or upload your client's confidential mockup to someone else's server.

Here is how to get a usable palette from any image in seconds — and how to get the part that actually matters to developers: the code.

What "extract a palette" really means

Extracting a palette means sampling an image and reducing its thousands of pixel colors down to a handful of representative, dominant ones — usually 3 to 10. Good tools use a quantization method like median cut, which repeatedly splits the color space along its widest axis so the final swatches genuinely reflect the image instead of grabbing random pixels.

The output you want is not just pretty squares. You want:

  • HEX codes you can drop into a design file
  • RGB / HSL values for fine-tuning
  • Code — CSS variables, SCSS, a Tailwind config, JSON — so the palette lands in your project without you retyping six hex codes by hand

That last part is where most free tools quietly stop.

The catch with the popular tools

Coolors is excellent, but its image-palette picker and advanced exports sit behind Coolors Pro. You can make palettes for free; pulling one from your own image and exporting it cleanly is the paid path.

Adobe Color extracts from images well, but it wants you signed into an Adobe account, and it is built to feed the Creative Cloud ecosystem rather than hand you a Tailwind config.

imagecolorpicker.com and similar sites work, but you upload your image to their server to do it — a non-starter if the image is an unreleased product shot or NDA client work.

None of these is bad. But if what you want is fast, private, and code-ready, they each make you trade something.

The faster path: extract locally in your browser

This is the gap Huepine fills. It is a color-palette extractor that runs 100% in your browser — your image never gets uploaded anywhere.

The flow is intentionally short:

  1. Open the app and drop in an image (PNG, JPG, or WEBP). Nothing uploads — the file is read locally on your machine.
  2. Choose 3-10 colors with a slider and hit Extract. Median-cut sampling pulls the dominant colors and sorts them by luminance, light to dark.
  3. Click any swatch to copy its HEX instantly. Click a swatch to also set it as the base for complementary, analogous, and triadic harmonies — handy when one color from the photo needs a supporting cast.
  4. Export. Get a labeled PNG palette strip to drop into a brief or handoff, or generate CSS variables, SCSS, a Tailwind color config, or JSON with HEX/RGB/HSL.

The extraction, the on-screen harmonies, and the PNG strip are free. The code exports — the CSS/SCSS/Tailwind/JSON a developer pastes straight into a project — unlock with a one-time lifetime license. No subscription, no account.

Why "nothing uploaded" matters more than it sounds

If you do client work, the image you are sampling is often something that does not exist publicly yet — a rebrand, a product render, an unannounced app screen. Every browser-based tool that processes your image on a server is a tiny data-handling question you would rather not answer. Huepine sidesteps it: the pixels are read in the page and never leave the tab. Close the tab and there is nothing to clean up.

Honest limits

It is built to be fast and local, so it is deliberately narrow:

  • One image at a time — no batch processing of a folder.
  • No cloud library — there is no account saving your past palettes; you export what you need and move on.
  • No sync — it lives in the browser tab, not across your devices.

If your workflow depends on a shared, cloud-synced palette library across a team, a heavier platform is the right call. If you just need the colors out of this image and into your code right now, that overhead is the thing you are trying to avoid.

The short version

To extract a color palette from an image and get real hex codes:

  1. Use a tool that samples with median cut and sorts by luminance.
  2. Make sure it gives you HEX/RGB/HSL, not just swatches.
  3. If the image is sensitive, use one that runs locally and uploads nothing.
  4. Get the palette out as code, not a screenshot you have to transcribe.
